The Dying Automotive Soul

POSTED: 2026-03-05

The ENGL 170 class dashboard is currently filled with discussions regarding how AI-driven generative tools are being used to "optimize" creative fields, but nowhere is this more visible—or more tragic—than in the current state of car design. We are witnessing the death of the automotive soul. Historically, cars were designed by humans with pens and clay—individuals who valued emotion, silhouette, and the visceral "feel" of a machine. Designers understood that a car is an extension of the driver’s personality, creating icons like the 3rd Gen Acura TL, which balanced sharp, aggressive lines with a stance that looked fast even when standing still. Today, however, AI-driven wind tunnel simulations and drag-coefficient algorithms have taken the lead, shifting the priority from aesthetic passion to mathematical perfection.

The result is a "flattening" of design where every car begins to look like the same generic, aero-efficient blob. When AI optimizes for a single data point—in this case, aerodynamic drag—it ignores the human desire for character and heritage. By connecting this to the class discussions on "Algorithmic Boringness," we can see that AI is not an assistant to the designer; it is a replacement for the artist. We are trading legendary lines for "optimized" pods that have no identity. This mirrors the concerns raised by my peers regarding AI’s role in art and literature: when you remove the human struggle and the "imperfection" of a hand-drawn line, you lose the soul of the work. A car should be a piece of moving art, not a calculated solution to a physics equation. When we let the computer hold the pen, we lose the very reason we fell in love with driving in the first place.
